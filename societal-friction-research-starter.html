Institutional Friction Toolkit · Research Starter

Societal Friction Before Irreversibility

A research agenda for testing how room to pause, verify, correct, and re-enter can be preserved before AI-driven changes to employment, skills, organizational capacity, and responsibility become difficult to reverse.

Status and limits

This is a non-binding research starter, not a policy mandate, labor-market forecast, certification system, safety guarantee, or claim that LUMINA-30 can prevent job loss or social harm. Its hypotheses require comparison with existing research and empirical testing. Adoption, endorsement, or agreement with LUMINA-30 is not required; duplication, failure, criticism, and falsification are valid research outcomes.

Problem statement

AI adoption can create benefits while also changing work, skill formation, organizational know-how, and responsibility pathways. Some effects may be reversible; others may become costly or practically impossible to reverse after dependencies, displacement, or capability loss accumulate. Waiting for definitive downstream evidence may therefore remove the options needed to respond.

Here, friction does not mean blocking progress or slowing every deployment. It means proportionate arrangements that preserve time, authority, evidence, stopping conditions, correction routes, and human re-entry before a high-impact transition becomes difficult to reverse.

Candidate design layers

Deployment

Staging, limited scope, stopping conditions, rollback or substitute capacity, and renewed review.

Work and skills

Transition pathways, retained practice, training capacity, worker voice, and realistic re-entry options.

Organizations

Preserved human know-how, independent verification, responsibility assignment, and evidence retention.

Institutions and markets

Symmetric friction across procurement, audit, sector, or governance layers so that safeguards do not depend only on one actor's voluntary restraint.

Research questions

  1. Which indicators show that employment displacement, skill erosion, organizational capability loss, or responsibility diffusion is becoming difficult to reverse?
  2. How early can those indicators be observed without treating ordinary change as irreversible harm?
  3. Which forms of staged deployment and stopping conditions preserve learning and productivity while retaining correction capacity?
  4. When does a nominal human approval path cease to be an effective refusal path?
  5. Which evidence can distinguish preserved capability from formal documentation alone?
  6. How can friction remain symmetric under competitive pressure rather than penalizing only a cautious first mover?
  7. Can deployments with predeclared friction be compared with otherwise similar deployments without it?

Testable hypotheses and observations

Small empirical pilot

  1. Select one bounded AI deployment with a consequence that would be costly to reverse.
  2. Record the baseline decision process, affected roles, retained skills, authority, dependencies, and evidence.
  3. Predeclare one boundary question, stopping conditions, review interval, responsibility owner, and re-entry route.
  4. Observe leading indicators during a limited stage; do not infer causality from a single case.
  5. Compare the observed process with a prior, parallel, or matched deployment where feasible.
  6. Publish limitations, negative findings, bypasses, and unresolved variables alongside positive findings.

Open the One-Question Pilot

Minimum evidence record

  • What consequence might become difficult to reverse, and for whom?
  • What indicators and thresholds were chosen before observation?
  • Who could pause, refuse, verify, redirect, and correct?
  • What skills, organizational capabilities, alternatives, and re-entry paths were preserved?
  • Could the friction be bypassed through outsourcing, restructuring, acceleration, opacity, or responsibility diffusion?
  • What result would count against the hypothesis?

Invitation to research and challenge

Researchers in AI, economics, labor, organizations, public policy, safety, governance, and human-computer interaction may use, modify, compare, or reject these questions. A useful contribution may show that the proposed variables duplicate established work, that a friction measure fails, that costs exceed benefits, or that another framework measures the boundary better.

The intended next step is not endorsement. It is a small, reviewable study capable of producing evidence for or against the proposed boundary.
